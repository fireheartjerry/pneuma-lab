\begin{abstract}
Papers reporting that persistent memory improves an LLM agent describe their
evaluations in terms of items: two thousand questions, a thousand tasks, a hundred
human ratings. Those items are not independent. The two thousand questions are drawn
from ten conversations; the thousand tasks from five websites; the hundred ratings
from a single simulation run. We audit thirty published agent-memory systems and
benchmarks against a rubric frozen before coding, and compute what effect size each
design could have detected across a range of clustering assumptions. Thirteen of
thirty-two coded rows cannot be bounded at all, because the paper does not state the
count of its own coarsest grouping. Only six state an effect precisely enough to
compare against any threshold, and five of those six fall below it at every
clustering assumption we report. We further distinguish two kinds of dependence that
the existing power literature does not model, because they are created by the
intervention rather than by the data: a memory pool that accumulates across an
evaluation couples every task to its predecessors, and a single induced artifact
scored on thousands of items provides one replication of the claim about that
artifact, not thousands. We report our own prior work under the identical rubric,
where it records the worst overstatement ratio in the frame.
\end{abstract}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Introduction}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------

An agent-memory paper reports how much it evaluated on. Mem0 reports two thousand
questions~\cite{chhikara_mem0_2025}. Agent Workflow Memory reports over a thousand
tasks~\cite{wang_agentworkflowmemory_2025}. Generative Agents reports a hundred human
rankings~\cite{park_generativeagents_2023}. These numbers establish that a result is
solid, and every confidence interval, significance test, and implicit power
calculation in the literature is computed against them.

The two thousand questions come from ten conversations. The thousand tasks come from
five websites. The hundred rankings all come from a single two-day simulation.

That benchmark items are correlated rather than independent is known, and correcting
for it changes model rankings~\cite{siska_robustness_2024}. That evaluation intervals
are often computed over the wrong quantity is known~\cite{miller_errorbars_2024,
bowyer_dontusetheclt_2025}. That a literature can be audited for what its designs
could detect is known---it is the shape of~\cite{card_littlepower_2020}, which found
NLP experiments broadly underpowered, and it has since been applied to leaderboard
rank resolution~\cite{kotawala_resolutiondiagnostics_2026} and to benchmark sample
complexity~\cite{huang_howmanytasks_2026}. None of that is ours, and we say so here
rather than in a related-work section on page four.

What has not been done is to point that instrument at \emph{agent memory}, where the
problem has a structural cause that does not exist in the literatures where these
methods were developed. In NLP benchmarking, item dependence is a property of how the
data was built. In agent memory it is additionally a property of \emph{the thing
being tested}.

Consider what a memory system claims. It claims that information acquired while
solving task $k-1$ improves performance on task $k$. That is the contribution. But
the standard inferential treatment of the same evaluation asserts that tasks are
independent samples. Both cannot hold. If memory transfers information across tasks
then the observations are coupled, and the coupling is proportional to how well the
memory works. \textbf{The stronger the claimed effect, the more severe the violation
of the assumption used to test it.}

A second case has the same character. Several systems induce a single artifact---a
skill document, a workflow library, a distilled experience set---and then score it on
thousands of items. Trace2Skill evolves one skill document from one evolving set and
reports up to 57.65 points from it across 2{,}649 test
instances~\cite{ni_trace2skill_2026}. For the claim \emph{this skill document works},
the number of independent replications is one.

We therefore distinguish three kinds of dependence, and only the first is what the
existing literature models:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Substrate dependence}---imposed by the benchmark, inherited by
        every system evaluated on it. LoCoMo's fifty conversations become fifty
        clusters for everyone who uses it, permanently~\cite{maharana_locomo_2024}.
    \item \textbf{Endogenous dependence}---created by a memory pool accumulating
        across the evaluation, so that task $k$ is conditioned on tasks
        $1 \ldots k-1$.
    \item \textbf{Treatment-artifact dependence}---where all scored observations
        share one induced artifact, so the effective replication count for the claim
        about that artifact is the number of artifacts.
\end{enumerate}

We audit thirty agent-memory systems and benchmarks against a rubric frozen before
any paper was read, compute what effect each design could have detected across a
range of clustering assumptions, and compare that to what each paper claims. The
result is less about power than we expected. \textbf{Only six of thirty-two coded
rows state an effect precisely enough to compare against any threshold at all.}
Thirteen cannot be bounded because the paper never states the count of its own
coarsest grouping. Of the six that can be checked, five fall below their own
detection threshold at every clustering assumption we report.

Our contributions are the audit, the taxonomy, a detectability surface reported as a
range rather than a point, and a released tool. \textbf{We do not claim that any
audited system fails to work.} We claim that most of these studies could not have
detected the effects they report, and that a majority do not report enough for a
reader to check. We include our own prior work in the audit under the identical
rubric, where it records the worst overstatement ratio in the frame.

\section{What the literature reports}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Frame}

Thirty systems and benchmarks, selected by a procedure fixed before any paper was
read: ten seeds, plus every system a seed compares against, plus every system cited
by a recent agent-memory survey~\cite{du_agentmemorysurvey_2026}. Two systems were
admitted by a recorded amendment after the original procedure was found to have
under-reached them; that amendment, and every other adjudication in this paper, is
dated and published with its rejected alternatives.

The frame stratifies into \textbf{system papers}, which claim a memory mechanism
helps, and \textbf{benchmark papers}, which claim nothing about their own memory but
impose their sample structure on everyone who evaluates against them. A benchmark
built from fifty conversations imposes fifty clusters on every downstream user,
permanently. We report the strata separately. We include our own prior work as a
third stratum of one.

\subsection{Coding}

Nine fields per paper, coded against a rubric frozen before the first paper was read.
Every non-numeric cell is backed by a verbatim quoted passage with a section locator,
and the full coding sheet is released.

\textbf{We report no inter-rater reliability coefficient.} Coding was performed by
one author with LLM assistance, and two instances of one model are not independent
raters. Printing an agreement statistic between them, in a paper about statistics
that do not mean what they appear to, would be self-refuting. Quote-backing is
offered instead and is stronger: a reader can check any cell against its source
rather than trusting a coefficient. Where a paper does not state something, the cell
reads \texttt{not\_stated}. It is never inferred, including where the intended value
seems obvious.

\subsection{What the coding found}

Reporting practice across the frame, scoped to these thirty papers and this date
range: \textbf{31 of 32 rows pre-specify no decision rule}, the exception being ours,
which was preregistered; \textbf{28 of 32 have no inert-content null arm}, so
comparisons confound the content of a memory with the apparatus that delivers it; and
\textbf{0 of 32 report uncertainty over their own coarsest grouping}. One paper
reports confidence intervals at all~\cite{shu_agentcl_2026}, and each interval sits
inside a single task stream, so it structurally cannot resample the unit that would
matter.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Three kinds of dependence}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Substrate.} Benchmark items nest. LoCoMo compiles 7{,}512 questions from
50 dialogues~\cite{maharana_locomo_2024}. MemBench generates 53{,}000 questions from
500 relation graphs~\cite{tan_membench_2025}. MemoryAgentBench states the design
choice outright: ``we deliberately design scenarios where multiple questions follow a
single context''~\cite{hu_memoryagentbench_2025}. This is the kind the existing
literature models. It is a property of the measurement substrate, it exists whether
or not anything is being tested, and it propagates.

\paragraph{Endogenous.} An agent-memory system accumulates a pool across the
evaluation. Memento initialises memory from scratch and stores trajectories into a
case bank across its validation set~\cite{zhou_memento_2025}. ReasoningBank evaluates
on a task stream where each query ``must be completed sequentially without access to
future ones''~\cite{ouyang_reasoningbank_2026}. Under such designs task $k$ is
conditioned on tasks $1 \ldots k-1$ through the pool, and the coupling is created by
the intervention under test. This produces a specific awkwardness: Memento's design
codes an overstatement ratio of $1.0$---the cleanest in the frame---for an evaluation
whose dependence is total, because a procedure that counts observations cannot see
coupling the system itself creates.

The general statement: \emph{``memory carries information across tasks'' and ``tasks
are independent evaluation samples'' are the same quantity, asserted in one direction
as the contribution and denied in the other as a statistical premise.}

\paragraph{Treatment-artifact.} Trace2Skill evolves one skill document from a
200-sample evolving set, then scores it across 2{,}649 held-out instances and reports
up to 57.65 points~\cite{ni_trace2skill_2026}. Every scored item shares that one
artifact. For the claim \emph{this document works}, $n = 1$. The analogy is a drug
trial that manufactures a single batch, administers it to 2{,}649 patients, and
reports 2{,}649 independent tests of the manufacturing process. The patients are
independent; the batch is not.

The three are independent of one another and can co-occur. Seven of the coded rows
carry a shared memory lifetime; several of those also draw from a clustered
substrate. We report each separately and do not combine them into a single corrected
number, because the data required to do so is not published.

\section{What a credible claim requires}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{We bound, we do not estimate}

The design effect is $\mathrm{DEFF} = 1 + (m-1)\rho$, giving
$n_{\mathrm{eff}} = n / \mathrm{DEFF}$~\cite{kish_surveysampling_1965}. Published
reports state $n$ and the cluster count, hence $m$---but never $\rho$.
\textbf{Substituting the cluster count for the observation count silently assumes
$\rho = 1$}, which is a worst-case bound rather than a correction.

We therefore report the minimum detectable effect across a grid,
$\rho \in \{0.05, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 1.0\}$ crossed with paired discordance
$d \in \{0.15, 0.25, 0.40\}$, as a range. No point estimate of effective sample size
appears anywhere in this paper. For a paired comparison on binary-scored items---the
usual case, since these papers compare a system against its own ablation on identical
items---with $D_i \in \{-1,0,1\}$ and discordance $d = P(D_i \neq 0)$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{MDE}(\rho_D) \approx
    \left(z_{1-\alpha/2}+z_{1-\beta}\right)
    \sqrt{\frac{d \cdot \mathrm{DEFF}_D(\rho_D)}{n}}
\end{equation}
The binding quantity is the discordance rate, not either system's accuracy, because
McNemar analyses only discordant pairs~\cite{mcnemar_correlatedproportions_1947}. We
use two-sided $\alpha = 0.05$ and $80\%$ power; one-sided would be defensible only
for a precommitted directional question, and 31 of 32 papers pre-specify no decision
rule at all.

\subsection{Non-identifiable designs}

Where all observations derive from one cluster, between-cluster variance and $\rho$
are not estimable. There is no identified design effect, no cluster-robust standard
error, and no minimum detectable effect for a claim generalizing beyond that
cluster~\cite{murray_grouprandomized_2004, eldridge_clusterrandomised_2012}. Such
designs are reported as \textbf{non-identifiable with their reason}, never assigned a
number. Generative Agents is the clearest case: 100 rankings, one simulation. The
honest statement is not that its effective sample size is one; it is that
population-level uncertainty cannot be estimated from these data.

\subsection{Verdict stability}

A verdict computed at one assumed $\rho$ and one assumed $d$ is a rendering of those
assumptions. We therefore report, for every checkable claim, whether its verdict
holds across the entire two-dimensional surface or flips somewhere, and where.

\section{Results}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Most claims cannot be checked at all}

Of 32 coded rows, 13 are non-identifiable---no stated grouping count, or a single
cluster---13 are identifiable but state no verifiable absolute effect, and 6 are
verdict-eligible. We observed four distinct reasons a row is not checkable.
\textbf{No magnitude stated}: MemoryBank~\cite{zhong_memorybank_2024} and
Think-in-Memory~\cite{liu_thinkinmemory_2023} both assert improvement in prose
without giving a number. \textbf{No unit count stated}: seven papers never report the
count of their own coarsest grouping. \textbf{Relative stated as absolute}: one
paper reports ``23.5\% and 11.3\% in success rate''~\cite{wang_inducingskills_2025};
the arithmetic identifies these as relative, and the absolute values are 7.7 and 4.1.
\textbf{Magnitude not recomputable}: Reflexion states ``an absolute
22\%''~\cite{shinn_reflexion_2023}, which two independent attempts could not
reproduce from the paper's own reported quantities.

A fifth is distinct from all four: \textbf{budget-mismatched comparison}. JARVIS-1's
``5 times'' pairs its own 60-minute result against a baseline's 20-minute
result~\cite{wang_jarvis1_2025}. The paper reports that baseline at 60 minutes
itself, where the ratio is 4.17.

\subsection{Our own result, under the identical rubric}

E-0 reports 3{,}141 evaluation instances drawn from 4 repositories---an overstatement
ratio of \textbf{785.2, the highest in the frame}, nearly four times the next. Its
reported statistic, $z = -16.03$, is computed over items; clustering by repository
deflates it to 2.53 at $\rho=0.05$ and 0.57 at $\rho=1$.

The direction matters. E-0's verdict was a failure---our own system lost to a
retry-count controller---so item-level inference inflated our confidence in a
\emph{negative} result. At defensible clustering we could not have concluded that
either. The same error inflates nulls as readily as positives, and an audit applying
its critique only to favourable findings would be practising the selective rigour it
documents.

\section{Related work}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------

\textbf{Power audits of a literature.} \cite{card_littlepower_2020} established this
paper's shape: harvest reported designs, compute what they could detect, report how
often the literature falls short. It treats items as independent, which is correct
for the single-endpoint NLP comparisons it studies and is precisely the assumption
that fails here.

\textbf{Non-independence in LLM evaluation.} \cite{siska_robustness_2024} showed that
correlation across test prompts is non-random and that accounting for it changes
model rankings. That is the closest peer-reviewed ancestor of our core observation.
It concerns correlation among prompts within a benchmark rather than hierarchical
nesting, and rankings rather than detectability.

\textbf{Clustered inference for evaluation.} \cite{miller_errorbars_2024} supplies
the statistical treatment; \cite{bowyer_dontusetheclt_2025} shows naive intervals
understate uncertainty in small evaluations; \cite{zeng_missingresponses_2025} applies
cluster-robust inference to multi-turn LLM evaluation.
\cite{kotawala_resolutiondiagnostics_2026} performs the closest execution of the
method we use, computing a real ICC correction and reporting that unresolved
adjacent-rank comparisons rise from four of nine to six of nine under clustering. It
analyses leaderboard rank comparisons, not published claims, and does not cover agent
evaluation.

\textbf{Variance in agent evaluation.} \cite{bjarnason_randomness_2026} measured
run-to-run variance over sixty thousand trajectories, finding single-run pass@1
varies by 2.2 to 6.0 points depending on which run is selected, and recommends power
analysis without performing one. \cite{huang_howmanytasks_2026} asks how many tasks
reproduce a benchmark decision, a reproduction question rather than a detectability
one.

\textbf{Controls in agent-memory evaluation.} \cite{wang_memdelta_2026} audits one
system with controlled baselines including a random-content retrieval arm, and
\cite{hajimiri_onlineskillmodules_2026} shows that several skill and memory modules do
not beat a token-matched vanilla agent. Both are direct prior art for the concern that
motivates this audit; neither computes detectability.

\section{Limitations}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------

\textbf{The method is not ours.} Detectability analysis
is~\cite{card_littlepower_2020}; clustered inference for evaluation
is~\cite{miller_errorbars_2024}; non-independence in LLM benchmarks
is~\cite{siska_robustness_2024}; the closest execution on real data
is~\cite{kotawala_resolutiondiagnostics_2026}. Our contribution is the corpus, the
taxonomy, and the observation that two of the three dependence kinds arise from the
intervention.

\textbf{The frame is thirty papers and one date range.} Every count describes that
frame. None is a claim about agent-memory research in general, and the sampling
procedure is published so a reader can disagree with a known quantity.

\textbf{The independent unit is a judgment.} Our rubric fixes a four-rule decision
procedure applied mechanically rather than by preference. Six rows carry a defensible
alternative unit; we publish both and report the verdict under each.

\textbf{$\rho$ is unobserved.} We bound rather than estimate, and the bound is wide.
For several papers the MDE range spans an order of magnitude. That width is honest
reporting of what public information supports; it is not precision.

\textbf{Six of thirty-two.} Most of the frame yields no verdict. This limits any
quantitative summary of the literature's detectability, and we resist reporting a
rate over the six as though it characterised the thirty.

\textbf{We are in the frame and we score worst.} Our own prior result carries the
highest overstatement ratio here, and its item-level inference inflated confidence in
a negative finding.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------

The question is not whether agent memory works. It is whether the published record
can tell us. On the evidence of thirty papers: mostly not---not because the field is
careless, but because the standard reporting conventions were built for evaluations
whose items are independent, and agent memory is the case where the intervention
itself makes them not.

A memory system that works is a memory system whose evaluation violates the
assumption used to test it. That is not a flaw anyone introduced. It is a structural
feature of testing something whose entire purpose is to carry information between the
things you are counting as independent.
